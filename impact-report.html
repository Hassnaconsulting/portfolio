01Executive Summary

At J+30, 6 of 8 participants have reached functional adoption — they use ChatGPT independently for at least 3 HR tasks per week without assistance. The two remaining participants (Pierre D. and Marc B.) are on a structured reinforcement plan and are expected to reach functional adoption by J+60.

This report documents what changed, why it changed, and what the program recommends for the next phase. The central finding: adoption barriers were human, not technical. The program addressed them as such.

02What We Measured and Why

This program tracks behavior change — not training completion. A participant who attended the workshop and completed the quiz but never opens ChatGPT at work has not adopted. A participant who now writes job descriptions in 5 minutes every day has.

Three measurement methods

A — Weekly self-declaration (J+7, J+14, J+21, J+30)
Each participant submitted a weekly log: which HR tasks they used ChatGPT for and approximately how much time they saved. This is the primary adoption signal.
B — Confidence self-assessment
At baseline and J+30, each participant rated their confidence 1–10: "I can use ChatGPT independently for this HR task without assistance." Perceived ability correlates strongly with sustained usage.
C — Direct observation (voluntary)
3 participants volunteered for a 20-minute observation session at J+28 to verify self-declared usage and identify blocking issues. Not included in scoring.

05Human Side Analysis — Why This Was Not a Training Problem

The most important finding of this program is not the time savings. It is the confirmation that adoption barriers were human, not technical. Every participant who struggled did so for a human reason — not because ChatGPT was difficult to use.

What the ADKAR baseline revealed

Before any tool training, the needs analysis identified 4 of 8 participants with moderate to high resistance. The specific barriers mapped to ADKAR dimensions:

  • Pierre D. and Marc B.: low Awareness (unclear why AI was relevant to their specific role) and low Desire (no perceived personal benefit) — the two deepest resistance dimensions
  • Léa F.: low Desire — skepticism about AI output quality. Resolved during M01 after first live demonstration
  • Julie P.: low Ability confidence — resolved through structured practice across M01–M03

What changed at the human level

The most significant behavioral shift came not from the tool training itself — it came from the moment participants experienced their first successful output on a real task. Desire follows concrete personal proof, not explanation of benefits.

Léa F. — the skeptic who adopted
"I was skeptical until I synthesized the 360° feedbacks in 3 minutes. That was the moment I understood." Score moved from 4.8 to 7.8 in 30 days.
Julie P. — the low-confidence participant
"I didn't believe I could do it. The first job description I produced was actually good. That changed everything." Score moved from 3.5 to 7.5.

What Pierre D. and Marc B. still need

Both need role-specific demonstrations that make ChatGPT personally relevant to their daily work — not generic HR use cases. The J+60 reinforcement plan addresses this directly.

Pierre D. — reinforcement plan
Customized prompts for learning design and training content creation — directly relevant to his Training Officer role. One-to-one session scheduled.
Marc B. — reinforcement plan
Specific use cases for payroll communications and HR policy summaries — his actual daily tasks. Simplified 3-prompt starter pack to be delivered before end of May.

08Conclusion

At J+30, this program has produced measurable behavior change — not training attendance, not quiz completion. 6 of 8 HR professionals now spend their time differently every day.

The two participants who have not yet reached functional adoption are not a program failure. They are its most important proof point. Their slower trajectory was predicted by the ADKAR baseline. Their reinforcement plan is specific to their actual barriers. Their path is defined.

This is what addressing the human side first produces in practice: a program that does not treat all participants the same — because they are not the same.
